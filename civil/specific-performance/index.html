Specific Performance in Pakistan

How specific performance works in Pakistan under the Specific Relief Act 1877: ready and willing, limitation, the notice, court fee, defences that succeed, and the drafting checklist.

Specific performance asks the court to force the seller to transfer property that was agreed to be sold. The buyer must prove a valid agreement, that he was ready and willing to perform, that he did perform or tendered performance, and that he asked for the transfer within limitation.

Key facts

Statute
Specific Relief Act 1877, section 12
Plaintiff must prove
Agreement, readiness and willingness, tender of price, and limitation
Limitation
Runs from the date fixed for performance; check the Limitation Act 1908 before drafting
Court fee
Ad valorem on the value of the property — check the provincial Court Fees Act
Most common defence
The buyer was never ready and willing, or the suit is time-barred
Alternative relief
Damages or refund, pleaded in the same suit

What must the buyer prove?

Four things: a concluded agreement, readiness and willingness, actual tender or an excuse for not tendering, and that the suit is within time.

  1. Agreement — written and signed, identifying the property precisely, with the price and the date fixed for performance.
  2. Readiness and willingness — money available, no legal bar on the buyer, and conduct consistent with wanting to complete.
  3. Tender — offer of the balance price to the seller, or proof of why no tender was possible.
  4. Notice — a written demand giving the seller a final opportunity before filing.
  5. Limitation — the date the clock started, calculated and pleaded in the plaint itself.
The evidence that decides these cases: bank statements or a sale of other assets showing the buyer had the money. A buyer who cannot show funds on the date of performance loses, even with a perfectly drafted agreement.

Which defences actually succeed?

Readiness and willingness, limitation, a defective or incomplete agreement, a third-party purchaser without notice, and the seller's own incapacity to transfer.

DefenceWhat the defendant must show
Not ready and willingThe buyer had no funds and never tendered the balance
Time-barredThe date fixed for performance is older than the limitation period
No concluded agreementEssential terms were still being negotiated
Third-party rightsA bona fide purchaser bought the property before the suit and without notice
ImpossibilityThe property was already lawfully transferred or is no longer the seller's

Courts treat the remedy as discretionary, so even a good claim can be refused where damages are the fairer outcome.

What is the drafting checklist?

Identify the property exactly, plead the agreement and its performance dates, plead readiness and willingness, attach the documents, and quantify the court fee.

  • Property description — plot number, area, scheme, survey number and boundaries, matching the revenue record.
  • Agreement details — date, price, payment schedule, date of performance, possession.
  • Performance pleaded — what was paid, what was tendered, when, and how.
  • Readiness and willingness — a specific paragraph with the evidence of funds.
  • Notice — date, mode and proof of service.
  • Relief — specific performance, or in the alternative refund with damages, plus costs and any injunction.
  • Court fee — correct ad valorem valuation, or the suit is returned at scrutiny.

Frequently asked questions

Is specific performance a right or a discretion?

It is a discretionary remedy. The court can refuse it and award damages or refund instead, which is why the alternative relief must always be pleaded.

Can it be claimed over agricultural land?

Depends on the transaction, the revenue record and any statutory restriction on transfer. Check the provincial law on agricultural land before drafting.

What if the seller has already sold to someone else?

The buyer's remedy may shift to damages, and the third party's status as a bona fide purchaser without notice becomes the central question. Act fast and consider an injunction.
