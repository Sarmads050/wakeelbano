Written Statement Format (Pakistan)

How to draft a written statement under Order VIII CPC in Pakistan: the paragraphs defendants forget, the admission-and-denial discipline, set-off and counter-claim, and filing mistakes.

A written statement is the defendant's reply to a plaint. It must deal with every allegation paragraph by paragraph, raise the legal objections first, and add a set-off or counter-claim only where the facts justify one. Order VIII CPC governs it.

Key facts

Governing law
Code of Civil Procedure 1908, Order VIII
Who files it
The defendant, after service of summons and the plaint
Time
Within the period fixed by the court on service — often 30 days
Shape
Paragraph-by-paragraph reply matching the plaint, plus separate grounds
Failure to file
The court may proceed ex parte or decide on the record
Verification
Required, with signature of the defendant and the advocate

What must a written statement contain?

Preliminary objections, the reply to each plaint paragraph, fresh facts relied on, any set-off or counter-claim, and a verification.

  1. Heading and title — court, case number, parties, and the title Written Statement.
  2. Preliminary objections — jurisdiction, limitation, maintainability, cause of action, valuation, non-joinder of parties.
  3. Reply on merits — numbered to match the plaint, not the defendant's story.
  4. Additional facts — facts the defendant will prove, each in its own paragraph.
  5. Set-off or counter-claim — only if a separate, quantified claim exists.
  6. Verification — that the contents are true to the defendant's belief, with the signature and place and date of signing.

Why do courts reject so many written statements?

Usually because allegations are answered vaguely instead of being admitted or denied, and because the reply does not follow the plaint paragraph by paragraph.

The rule to remember: silence is not a denial. An allegation that is not specifically denied or expressly admitted is likely to be taken as admitted. Every paragraph needs one of three answers — admitted, denied, or not admitted for want of knowledge.
  • Do not write a general denial of the entire plaint.
  • Do not answer with a legal argument where a fact was alleged.
  • Do not raise a set-off without a figure and a basis.
  • Do not leave preliminary objections buried in the middle of the merits.

What is the difference between a set-off and a counter-claim?

A set-off defends with a reciprocal debt; a counter-claim makes a claim of its own, and needs court fees and separate relief.

FeatureSet-off (Order VIII rule 6)Counter-claim (Order VIII rule 6-A)
PurposeExtinguish or reduce the plaintiff's claimObtain relief against the plaintiff
EffectDefence onlyTreated as a separate suit
Court feeGenerally not on the set-offRequired, like a plaint
RiskLost if not pleadedLost if not pleaded in the reply, though the court has power to allow later

What are the most common drafting mistakes?

Mismatched paragraph numbering, missing verification, no date or place, inconsistent facts against the earlier reply, and unsigned annexures.

  • Paragraph numbers that do not correspond with the plaint — the court cannot follow the reply.
  • Verification that does not state what is admitted and what is denied.
  • Facts pleaded without any witness or document to prove them.
  • Raising limitation in the merits instead of in the preliminary objections.
  • Copying a format from another case, leaving another party's name or facts inside.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if the written statement is not filed in time?

The court may decide the suit without it, or may allow filing subject to costs and conditions. Time limits are applied strictly in commercial cases; always check the order passed in your specific matter.

Can a written statement be amended later?

Yes, the court may allow amendment under Order VI rule 17, but not to change the case completely or where it is barred by limitation. Amendment is discretionary.

Does a counter-claim need separate court fees?

Yes. A counter-claim is treated as a plaint for the purposes of the Civil Courts Ordinance 1962 and the Court Fees Act, and relief in it is independent of the defence.
